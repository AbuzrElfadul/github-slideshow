### [0:12 – 1:10] RUNG 1 — COUNTING

> **VISUAL:** sheep hopping a fence; tally marks scratched into a cave wall.

**VO:** Maths starts with one idea: things can be counted.

A shepherd with forty sheep doesn't need to know what "forty" means. He needs one pebble per
sheep. Sheep go out, pebbles go in a bag. Sheep come back, pebbles come out. Bag empty at the
end of the day? Nobody got eaten.

That's the foundation. Matching one thing to one other thing. Every equation, every proof, every
terrifying symbol you're about to see is that same idea wearing a more expensive costume.

Eventually we gave the pebbles names — one, two, three. The natural numbers.

And almost immediately, they broke.

---

### [1:10 – 2:20] RUNG 2 — THE NUMBERS WE HAD TO INVENT

> **VISUAL:** a number line growing left past zero, then splitting into fractions.

**VO:** Here's the break. Three minus five.

In sheep, that's nonsense — there's no such thing as negative two sheep. But if three is what
you have and five is what you owe, then negative two is suddenly the most important number in
your life. So we invented it.

Then: what's half a sheep? Fractions. Then something worse. Take a square, one metre on each
side, and draw the diagonal. That length — the square root of two — cannot be written as a
fraction. Not "we haven't found it yet". It's provably impossible. The Greeks worked this out
and were so disturbed by it that, according to legend, the man who proved it was thrown into the
sea.

He was right. And the numbers kept coming. Zero, which took humanity thousands of years to
accept as an actual number rather than a gap. Pi, which never repeats and never ends. And *i* —
the square root of negative one — a number we openly made up because we needed it, and which now
runs your national power grid.

Watch the pattern, because it's the whole history of the subject: every time maths hit a wall, it
didn't stop. It invented a new kind of number and walked straight through.

---

### [2:20 – 3:30] RUNG 3 — ALGEBRA

**VO:** Arithmetic is when you know the numbers. Algebra is when you don't.

*x* plus three equals seven. You don't know *x*. But you know the **relationship** — and a
relationship turns out to be enough. Because whatever you do to one side of an equation, you do
to the other, and it stays true. Subtract three from both sides. *x* is four.

That's the whole trick. Every algebra problem you have ever hated is that one move, repeated
until you're tired.

But algebra was never really about finding *x*. It's about the fact that you can reason
rigorously about something you cannot see. Nobody has ever weighed the Earth. We know its mass
to five decimal places — because we know the relationships it has to everything else.

---

### [3:30 – 4:50] RUNG 4 — PROOF

> **VISUAL:** five short axioms typed onto papyrus, then an explosion of branching theorems.

**VO:** Around 300 BC, a man named Euclid did something genuinely insane.

He wrote down five rules so obvious they're almost embarrassing. You can draw a straight line
between any two points. You can extend it forever. That kind of thing.

Then, from *only those five rules*, he derived four hundred and sixty-five theorems. The entire
architecture of flat space, built out of five sentences.

This is where maths invented **proof** — the idea that you can start from things everyone
already agrees on and be *forced*, step by unavoidable step, into conclusions nobody expected.
Not evidence. Not "it's worked the last ten thousand times". Proof. And once something is
proven, it is true forever, everywhere, for everyone.

That's why maths is not like any other subject. Physics gets *revised*. Maths only ever gets
*added to*. Every theorem Euclid proved is still true today and will still be true after the
Sun burns out.

Oh — and that fifth rule, the one about parallel lines never meeting? Two thousand years later,
mathematicians threw it out purely to see what would happen. They got a consistent geometry
where parallel lines *do* meet. Everyone agreed it was a beautiful, pointless game.

Then Einstein used it to describe the actual shape of the universe. Hold onto that.

---

### [4:50 – 6:05] RUNG 5 — FUNCTIONS

> **VISUAL:** a cartoon machine — hopper on top, chute at the bottom. `3` in, `9` out.

**VO:** A function is a machine. One thing goes in, exactly one thing comes out. Put in three,
get out nine. That's the squaring machine.

Sounds trivial. It changed everything — because once you have functions, maths stops describing
*things* and starts describing *change*. Temperature as a function of time. Position as a
function of time. Your bank balance as a function of the subscriptions you forgot to cancel.

And you can draw one. Graph a function and you see its entire life in a single picture — every
possible input and output at once. Straight lines grow steadily. Parabolas curve. And
exponentials are the ones that destroy your intuition entirely.

Fold a sheet of paper in half forty-two times and it reaches the Moon. That isn't a joke or a
trick. That's just doubling, done more times than your brain is willing to imagine.

Humans are catastrophically bad at exponentials. That's essentially what a pandemic is — an
exponential curve, versus a species that thinks in straight lines.

---

### [6:05 – 7:50] RUNG 6 — CALCULUS

**VO:** Right. Calculus. The one with the reputation.

Here's the honest version. Calculus answers two questions — and the punchline is that they turn
out to be the same question, backwards.

**Question one: how fast is something changing right now?** Not average speed over an hour —
speed at this exact instant. Problem: an instant has zero duration, and distance divided by zero
time is meaningless. So Newton and Leibniz cheated, beautifully. Don't use an instant. Use a
tiny interval. Then a smaller one. Then smaller again, and watch what number your answer is
heading toward. That's a **limit**, and the number it arrives at is the **derivative**. The
slope of a curve at a single point.

**Question two: you know the speed at every instant — how far did you travel?** Slice the
journey into a million tiny pieces. Assume the speed is constant inside each piece. Multiply,
then add them all up. Now make the slices infinitely thin. That's an **integral** — the area
underneath a curve.

And here is the result that makes it a subject rather than two tricks. The Fundamental Theorem
of Calculus: those two operations undo each other exactly. Slicing-and-adding is the precise
opposite of finding-the-slope. Two completely different questions, secretly one machine.

Everything modern runs on this. Rocket trajectories. Drug dosages. Climate models. Every
prediction about anything that changes. Newton didn't invent calculus to ruin your Thursday
afternoons — he invented it because he wanted to know where the planets would be, and nothing
that existed could tell him.

---

### [7:50 – 9:05] RUNG 7 — PROBABILITY

**VO:** For most of its history, maths dealt in certainty. Then gamblers turned up with actual
money on the line, and maths had to learn how to be unsure.

Probability is counting again — count the outcomes you want, divide by all the outcomes there
are. Flip two coins: four possible results, one of which is two heads. One in four.

Then it gets strange fast. Put twenty-three people in a room and it's *more likely than not*
that two of them share a birthday. That feels wrong. It isn't — because you're not comparing
yourself against twenty-two people. You're comparing every possible pair, and there are two
hundred and fifty-three of those.

And then the big one: **Bayes' theorem.** A test is ninety-nine percent accurate. You test
positive for a disease that one person in ten thousand has. Your chance of actually having it is
not ninety-nine percent. It's roughly one percent. Because the disease is rare and false
positives are not — so almost everyone who tests positive is a false positive.

The test result doesn't *replace* what you knew before. It **updates** it.

That single idea — update, don't replace — is the foundation of modern statistics, modern
medicine, and every AI system currently pretending to understand you.

---

### [9:05 – 10:20] RUNG 8 — VECTORS AND MATRICES

**VO:** Quickly, because this one is everywhere and nobody teaches it early enough.

A **vector** is just a list of numbers. Your height, weight and age is a vector. A pixel's red,
green and blue values is a vector. A *word*, inside a language model, is a vector — a list of a
few thousand numbers that encodes what that word means.

A **matrix** is a machine that transforms vectors. Rotate them. Stretch them. Squash them.

And when you stack a few million of those transformations on top of each other, and then tune
all the numbers until the output is useful — congratulations, you've built a neural network.
That's it. That's what's under the bonnet of every AI you've ever used. Colossal piles of
multiplication and addition, performed on lists of numbers.

Linear algebra is why your phone recognises your face and your GPS knows where you are. It is
the least glamorous entry on this list and it quietly runs the modern world.

---

### [10:20 – 11:50] RUNG 9 — INFINITY

> **VISUAL:** two infinite lists side by side, being paired off with arrows.

**VO:** Now we go somewhere genuinely strange.

How many whole numbers are there? Infinite. How many *even* numbers? Also infinite — but surely
fewer, right? You just threw half of them away.

No. Pair them off. One with two. Two with four. Three with six. Every whole number gets exactly
one even number, forever, and nothing is left over on either side. Same size. You deleted half
the numbers and the set didn't shrink. That is what infinity does to your intuition.

Fine — so all infinities are the same size?

Also no. Cantor proved it, and the proof is short enough to do here. Take every number between
zero and one. Suppose you could write them all in a list. Now build a brand new number like
this: make its first digit different from the first number's first digit. Make its second digit
different from the second number's second digit. Keep going down the diagonal forever.

The number you've just built differs from *every single number on that list*, in at least one
place. So it wasn't on the list. But the list was supposed to contain all of them.

There are more numbers between zero and one than there are whole numbers in total. Infinities
come in different sizes. Cantor's own colleagues called his work a disease infecting mathematics.
He was right, and the fight over it destroyed him.

### [11:50 – 13:20] RUNG 10 — INCOMPLETENESS

**VO:** So by around 1900, mathematics is the most reliable structure human beings have ever
built. And mathematicians decide to finish the job properly: write down one perfect set of
starting rules, and derive every true mathematical statement from them. Complete. Consistent.
Sealed. Done forever.

A twenty-five-year-old named Kurt Gödel killed it.

His move was to make mathematics talk about *itself*. He found a way to assign a number to every
mathematical statement — so a statement becomes a number, and statements *about* statements
become ordinary arithmetic.

Then he constructed one specific statement which says, in effect: **this statement cannot be
proven within this system.**

Now look at what that does. If you *can* prove it, then it's false — and your system is proving
false things, which means it's broken. If you *can't* prove it, then what it says is true — and
your system contains a true statement it can never reach.

Either your mathematics is inconsistent, or it is incomplete. Those are the only two options.
And you can't patch it by adding more rules, because the same trick works on whatever new system
you build.

There are true things that cannot be proven. Permanently. That isn't a limit on how clever we
are. It's a property of truth itself.

---

### [13:20 – 14:30] RUNG 11 — WHY ANY OF THIS WORKS

**VO:** One last thing, and it's the strangest of all.

Non-Euclidean geometry was an idle curiosity for a hundred years. Then it turned out to be the
shape of spacetime.

Number theory was, for centuries, the proudest example of *useless* mathematics — its most
famous champion boasted it would never have a practical application of any kind. It is now the
only reason your bank details survive the journey across the internet.

Complex numbers were invented as a bookkeeping trick for an equation nobody could otherwise
solve. They are now indispensable to quantum mechanics — the literal description of what matter
is.

The physicist Eugene Wigner called this "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics". We
invent abstract structures essentially for fun, and then decades later the universe turns out to
have been running on them the whole time. Nobody knows why.

Either the universe is fundamentally mathematical — or our brains can only perceive the parts of
it that are. Pick whichever one lets you sleep.

**VO:** So that's the lot. Counting. Numbers we had to invent. Algebra. Proof. Functions.

Maths isn't a subject. It's a four-thousand-year argument about what *has* to be true — and it's
