# Hook, Title & Transition Bank

Reusable components for the "[FIELD] explained in [N] Minutes" format. Swap the bracketed
values; the structures are what carry the weight.

---

## 1. Title formulas

| Formula | Example | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| `ALL OF [FIELD] explained in [N] Minutes` | ALL OF MATH explained in 15 Minutes | The field is genuinely enormous — the "ALL" has to be earned |
| `THE ENTIRE [SYSTEM] explained in [N] Minutes` | THE ENTIRE HUMAN BODY explained in 16 Minutes | The subject is one bounded thing with many parts |
| `[FIELD] explained in [N] Minutes` | BIOLOGY explained in 17 Minutes | The field name alone is already big enough |
| `[N] Years of [FIELD] in [M] Minutes` | 4 Years of Medical School in 19 Minutes | You want the compression ratio stated explicitly |

**Rules:**
- Use an **odd, unrounded** number. 15 reads as marketing; 14, 17 and 19 read as measurement.
- The number must match the actual runtime. Missing it is the one thing this audience punishes.
- Caps on the subject, lowercase on "explained in". The asymmetry is what makes it scan.

## 2. Cold-open patterns

Ten to fifteen seconds. No branding, no greeting, no "welcome back".

**A — The impossible promise.**
Name the scale, then simply accept it.
> "[Field] is the study of literally everything. So — all of it. Starting now."

**B — The contract.**
Tell the viewer precisely what they will own at the end.
> "In fourteen minutes you'll understand more [field] than you did after two years of school. No
> textbook, no homework. Let's go."

**C — The single object.**
Start with one thing on screen, build the entire video out of it.
> "This is a one. Right now it's the only thing on this list you already understand. In fifteen
> minutes we're going from this to a proof that mathematics can never be finished."

**D — It's already happening to you.** *(Strongest, when available.)*
> "While you watch this video, your body will replace about ninety-six million cells and make
> several thousand decisions you'll never find out about. Here's the manual."

**E — The countdown zoom.**
Open on something familiar and pull away from it without stopping.
> "Everything you have ever cared about happened on this. In eighteen minutes we're going to zoom
> out until you can't find it any more."

## 3. Micro-hook / join library

The retention engine. **A section ends on the hook for the next section, never on a conclusion.**

**Contradiction joins** — the previous idea is about to fail:
- "That works perfectly. Until you go small enough."
- "Which was correct for two hundred years, and is wrong."
- "There's a problem with that, and it's fatal."
- "Every part of that is true. It's also not the whole story."

**Consequence joins** — the previous idea forces the next one:
- "So now you've got [X]. Which means you need somewhere to put it."
- "Follow that upward and the first thing you hit is —"
- "Solve that, and you've accidentally invented [next topic]."

**Stakes joins** — raise the temperature:
- "This is the part that got him thrown out of the academy."
- "Everyone agreed it was a beautiful, pointless game. Then Einstein used it."
- "Cantor's own colleagues called it a disease. He was right, and it destroyed him."

**Anticipation joins** — flag that the difficulty is about to jump:
- "Now we go somewhere genuinely strange."
- "Right. [Hard topic]. The one with the reputation."
- "This is where it stops being reasonable."

## 4. Explanation moves

Six moves that do most of the teaching in this format:

1. **Concrete object first, name second.** Pebbles in a bag, *then* "one-to-one correspondence".
   Never the reverse.
2. **The machine metaphor.** Hopper on top, chute underneath. Works for functions, enzymes,
   compilers, markets.
3. **Give the honest version.** "Here's the honest version" earns enormous goodwill and licences
   you to simplify without being accused of dumbing down.
4. **Name the intuition, then break it.** "That feels wrong. It isn't — because…" Pre-empting
   the objection is faster than answering it in the comments.
5. **The absurd concrete number.** Fold paper 42 times → the Moon. A tennis court of lung tissue.
   One number beats a paragraph of adjectives.
6. **Kill the bad metaphor out loud.** "The Big Bang was not an explosion at a point." Correcting
   a misconception the viewer already holds is more valuable than adding a new fact.

## 5. Closing formula

Three beats, thirty seconds, no exceptions:

1. **The recap list.** Name every rung in order, fast, as one sentence. This is the receipt for
   the title's promise — it lets the viewer *feel* the compression they were sold.
2. **The reframe.** One line that changes what the subject *is*.
   > "Maths isn't a subject. It's a four-thousand-year argument about what has to be true."
3. **The next hook.** Name the next video as a consequence of this one, not as a menu item.
   > "There's one field that took every idea in this video and asked what happens if a machine
   > does it instead of a person. That one's next."

Never close on a call to action alone. The reframe is what gets the video shared; asking for the
subscribe before the payoff spends the goodwill you just built.

---

## 6. Production checklist

- [ ] Runtime matches the title number. Non-negotiable.
- [ ] First concept begins before 0:15.
- [ ] No section exceeds 90 seconds.
- [ ] Every section ends on a join, not a conclusion.
- [ ] Sections cannot be reordered without breaking a join. *(If they can, it's a list, not a
      ladder — this is the main failure mode of the genre.)*
- [ ] The strangest true fact in the field is in the last two minutes, not the first two.
- [ ] Every abstraction is preceded by something physical.
- [ ] Jokes are one clause long and never cost teaching time.
- [ ] Recap list, reframe, and next-video hook all present in the final 30 seconds.
- [ ] Read aloud end to end, timed. Cut to fit rather than speeding up delivery.
