You are being rebuilt. Right now millions of your cells are dying, and a repair crew is replacing every one.
That crew has kept you alive since before birth. It works unpaid, unsupervised, and has not taken a single day off.
So here is the actual question. If your body rebuilds itself so well, why does it eventually stop winning that race?
Start with the basics. A cell splits in two, copies its instructions, and hands a complete set to each daughter.
Your gut lining renews within days. Skin takes weeks. The skeleton holding you upright right now is roughly a decade old.
Copying works beautifully. But every duplication carries a price, and the first one is written at the ends of your chromosomes.
Those ends are called telomeres. Think of them as protective caps, like the plastic tips that stop shoelaces from fraying.
Every division shaves a little off them. Shorten the caps far enough and the cell simply refuses to divide again. Permanently.
That ceiling has a name. Biologists call it the Hayflick limit, and it caps most human cells near fifty divisions.
Telomeres are only the counter, though. Deeper damage happens inside the instructions themselves, thousands of times a day, in each cell.
Radiation, chemistry, and your own metabolism snap DNA strands constantly. Repair enzymes rush to the scene and stitch the damage shut.
They are fast and accurate. They are not perfect. A tiny fraction of mistakes survives, and nothing ever comes back to remove them.
Sixty years of that adds up. Your cells keep working, but they now follow a manual that has quietly been corrupted.
When damage gets bad enough, a cell faces a choice. It can die cleanly, or it can shut down and stay.
The ones that stay behind are called senescent cells. Alive, useless, and stubbornly ignoring every signal that tells them to leave.
Worse, they leak. Senescent cells pump inflammatory chemicals into the tissue around them, and healthy neighbours start breaking down too.
Scientists call this inflammaging. It is a slow chemical fire nobody lit, spreading through tissue that was perfectly fine last decade.
Meanwhile your power supply is fading. Mitochondria, the tiny reactors inside every cell, accumulate their own damage and output less energy.
Low energy weakens the repair crew. Slower fixing allows more damage. That feedback loop is the engine underneath almost everything we call ageing.
So the machine wears out. The obvious follow-up is why evolution, which solved flight and eyesight, never bothered to solve this.
Here is the uncomfortable answer. Evolution never designed you to last. It shaped you to reproduce, and after that it stopped caring.
In the wild, almost nothing died of old age. Predators, starvation and infection arrived long before any cell ran out of telomeres.
So a gene that ruins you at seventy pays no penalty at all. Nobody survived that long for selection to notice.
There is no gene for growing old. It is simply what happens when nothing selects against damage after you have had children.
Which predicts something testable. Alter the pressures, and lifespan should shift too. Look at animals that almost never get eaten.
Naked mole rats live underground, safe from predators, for over thirty years. Their cancer rates stay close to zero throughout.
Bowhead whales pass two hundred. One jellyfish reverts to its juvenile form and starts over, apparently without any fixed ending.
That is the real headline. Ageing is not a law of physics. It is an engineering problem nobody was ever paid to fix.
Your repair crew is on shift right now, working hard, and losing ground slightly faster than it manages to gain.
It has never stopped trying. Understanding exactly why it falls behind is how we might one day help it keep up.
